\begin{abstract}
This annex proposes a broad luxury tax as a new own EU resource, bundled with the abolition of VAT refunds that would also generate additional revenue for member states, mostly from personal (portable) luxury goods. Both components are motivated by the finding that raising a euro of revenue via increases in taxes on luxury (such as via an EU-wide luxury EU own resource and the VAT refund abolition) would cost the EU countries substantially less than one euro in economic burden. This is because national incentives for luxury taxation are distorted downwards as countries compete for internationally mobile luxury shoppers via low taxes on portable luxury goods and for wealthy residents via low luxury taxes on residence-based products (e.g. luxury cars). We therefore propose to implement a new own EU resource as a luxury excise, modeled on existing luxury excises such as those on cars in Denmark, where an ad valorem rate is charged on the value exceeding a category-specific per-item price threshold. We propose a uniform ad valorem rate across categories as a focal point to facilitate negotiations. A 20\% rate could raise in the order of EUR $45$ billion per year for the EU budget. For each category of luxury, a luxury tax channeled to the EU restricted to the category would create a disproportionate burden for some countries, as proxied by the share of the tax revenue collected divided by the share of EU GDP. However, the proposed broad design (including luxury cars, portable luxury goods, yachts, luxury alcohol) leads to a substantially more even distribution of the collected taxes relative to GDP. Some of the major countries that would still collect a share of the revenue from the luxury EU own resource substantially exceeding their GDP share (France, Italy, Spain) are likely to particularly benefit from the coordinated abolition of VAT refunds which could raise up to EUR 4 billion in revenue EU-wide. % \footnote{} AF: would be great to get an estimate of the gain for each country's budget of this abolition TODO
This is because France, Italy and Spain compete strongly for internationally mobile luxury shoppers and would collect a large share of the additional tax revenue from VAT refund abolition. Overall, the package could therefore have a chance to garner the required unanimity among EU member states, where the smaller countries bearing a disproportionate burden could be compensated by slightly lowering their GNI-based required contribution. The proposed reform seems feasible. It is also highly desirable from an efficiency perspective and from a distributional justice perspective. We provide economic modeling results suggesting that a EU-wide additive luxury tax rate of 20\% is justified on the grounds of status externalities and signaling alone. In addition, luxury taxation can increase welfare by partially making up for the lack of progressivity in income and wealth taxation caused by tax competition, while not aggravating the latter if (as proposed) the tax revenue is not retained by the countries but instead directed to the EU. All this is achieved without weakening incentives to save or invest and without suppressing philanthropy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

There are strong economic reasons (reviewed briefly below) for why taxing luxury goods at a higher rate than other goods will raise social welfare. However, if countries decide individually on their luxury good taxes then there are strong tax competition forces pushing luxury good taxation down. For portable luxury goods, countries have an incentive to lower luxury good taxes to attract and retain shoppers, thereby gaining other indirect tax revenue from their non-refundable spending on on-site consumption of other goods and services.  For non-portable luxury goods that are taxed based on residence (e.g. luxury cars), there is an incentive for countries to lower the tax rate in order to attract more (internationally mobile) high earners and high wealth individuals. In the case of the post-Brexit abolition of VAT refunds in the UK, these spillover effects became very visible for the case of portable  luxury goods whose sales shifted substantially to other countries, including continental Europe. % AF: source, specify 'substantially' (give a number in footnote) TODO I think it's important to document the UK case since the EU proposal shares a lot of similarities

This suggests that luxury good taxes are promising as an EU own resource, given that such a common tax could correct a distortion created by tax competition. Even if individual EU countries find it optimal for themselves to not introduce luxury good taxes, they might still find it in their interest to vote in favor of an EU-wide luxury tax, given that this will prevent them from losing shoppers or residents to other EU countries. Put differently, each EU country benefits from other EU countries' luxury good taxation via the resulting displacement of  %increased 
shoppers/residents to them. Thus, voting for an EU-wide luxury tax is more attractive.

We will argue that there are advantages to the "bundled approach" of introducing an EU-wide luxury tax to fund the EU budget as compared to the "separated approach" of introducing mandatory minimal luxury taxes and increasing the EU budget via the traditional GNI-based contributions. In other words, we will argue that the two problems, "tax competition leading to undertaxation of luxury" and "unanimity constraints leading under-provision of funding for the EU" can best be addressed via a single mechanism, namely an EU-wide luxury tax to fund the EU budget. 

There are two core arguments in favor of the bundled approach. Firstly, the bundled approach avoids, in contrast to the separated approach, aggravating tax competition for people with high income and wealth. Under the separated approach, attracting such people would be made more valuable for each country to the extent that the mandatory minimal luxury taxes increase the tax revenues that such people yield. Thus countries would have increased unilateral incentives to further lower taxes on high income and wealth. Under the bundled approach, no such aggravation of (income and wealth) tax competition would result. The reason for this is that each country would be indifferent about which would collect the EU-wide luxury tax, given that all the revenue would go to the EU budget anyway.

The first argument in favor of the bundling approach that we just sketched shows that the aggregate welfare gains achievable via the bundled approach are greater than under the separated approach. This is per se desirable and it would also make it easier to get unanimity. We now sketch a second argument suggesting that even if we neglect this welfare gain via avoided aggravation of tax competition the bundled approach might be more likely to get unanimity among EU member states.

This second argument in favor of bundling can be stated very generically: suppose we compare having votes on two reforms separately or on the combination of the two\footnote{Note that in our case, the bundled approach is, by the first argument, likely to yield welfare improvement over the separated approach and so the bundled approach is not just a combination of two components of the separated approach. We assume away this effect here just for analytical clarity.}. If each of the separate reforms garner unanimity then so should the combination. However, the converse does not hold.

We propose a single EU-wide indirect tax, levied at a uniform ad valorem rate on the part of an item's price that exceeds a category-specific threshold, assessed on the first supply to a final consumer or on importation, and collected by VAT-registered vendors through the existing VAT return. Its yield accrues to the Union budget as an own resource. We refer to it as the European luxury surcharge.

\subsection{Formal definition}

\begin{definition}[European luxury surcharge]
\label{def:surcharge}
The surcharge is defined by the following elements.

\emph{(i) Scope.} A set of product categories $\mathcal{C}$ gets enumerated. 

\emph{(ii) Thresholds.} Each category $c \in \mathcal{C}$ carries a threshold $\bar{p}_c$ which can be either 0 or positive. If positive, it is expressed as an amount per item before any taxes.

\emph{(iii) Rate.} A single rate $t^{\mathrm{EU}}$ applies uniformly across all categories on the value exceeding the threshold.

\emph{(iv) Collection.} The surcharge is declared and paid on the VAT return of the person liable, in a separate box, and is subject to the same registration, invoicing, record-keeping and audit obligations as value added tax.

\emph{(v) Attribution.} Amounts collected are attributed to the member state of taxation and transferred to the Union budget under the Own Resources Decision.
\end{definition}


\subsection{Thresholds}
\label{sec:thresholds}

Some thresholds could be set to 0, e.g. for jewelry containing specific precious stones. For many product categories, including cars, positive per item thresholds are essential.

Specific proposals for thresholds could be anchored in values already in use elsewhere. Korea's
Individual Consumption Tax applies to watches and bags above roughly \euro{}1{,}300 and to
jewellery, fur and leather goods above roughly \euro{}3{,}200. France's \emph{taxe forfaitaire sur
les objets pr\'ecieux} applies to jewellery, watches, art and antiques above \euro{}5{,}000 per
item, with the threshold assessed object by object except where the objects form a coherent set.
India's tax collected at source on luxury goods applies above roughly \euro{}10{,}000, uniformly
across its notified list of watches, art, collectibles, yachts, bags and footwear. For accommodation, Florence's \emph{imposta di soggiorno} is assessed directly against the
net price per guest per night, in bands whose highest begins at \euro{}200.

For motor cars the comparators are more dispersed: Australia's Luxury Car Tax threshold stands at
roughly \euro{}46{,}000, rising to roughly \euro{}69{,}000 for zero-emission vehicles; Canada's at
roughly \euro{}65{,}000; China's at roughly \euro{}110{,}000; and Greece's at \euro{}17{,}000.
Denmark's registration tax, the closest European analogue to a graduated luxury rate on vehicles,
reaches its 85 per cent bracket at roughly \euro{}8{,}200 and its top 150 per cent bracket at roughly
\euro{}25{,}500, once its VAT-inclusive base is restated net of VAT. Canada applies a separate and
much higher threshold of roughly \euro{}156{,}000 to vessels.

\subsection{Tax design}

\paragraph{The case for a uniform tax rate}

Purely from the perspective of aggregate welfare, it might be optimal to substantially differentiate the tax rates on different luxury goods, to the extent that they differ in the degree to which their consumption is motivated by status and the degree to which their consumption is correlated with income and wealth. However, a proposal for a uniform additive EU-wide tax rate as a new EU own resource, might be more easily adopted in a simple form with a uniform rate. The burden of taxes on luxury cars will disproportionately fall on Germany, whereas the burden of taxes on portable luxury goods will disproportionately fall on France. Hence negotiations about rate differentiation will have to some extent a zero-sum character. Having a uniform rate could be a reasonable focal point. Moreover, a uniform rate might lead to a relatively balanced distribution of tax burdens, as the estimates in section \ref{tax revenue estimates by eu member states} suggest.

\paragraph{Abolishing the VAT refunds}

We propose to combine the introduction of the European luxury surcharge with an EU-wide abolition of VAT refunding. VAT refunds mostly concern luxury goods, as only for high-value goods it is worth it for consumers to carry them home. 

\paragraph{Adoption}

Both of the proposed reforms, i.e. the EU-wide luxury excise and the VAT refund abolition, require unanimity among EU member states.

\paragraph{The (absence of) international legal/legitimacy issues}

There appear to be no papers or reports questioning the legality of abolishing VAT refunds. Moreover, when the UK abolished its VAT refund no other countries opposed it.

\section{The case for luxury taxation}

\subsection{The progressivity motive}

Luxury consumption is correlated with high income. As a result, luxury taxation is progressive and thus, to the extent that the status quo tax system is insufficiently progressive, luxury taxation looks promising. However, a standard objection to this argument is that the status quo tax system is a result of historical learning and societal deliberation, trading off the benefits of progressivity with its costs. Progressive luxury taxation reduces the work incentives in a way similar to how a progressive income tax does. It also makes it less attractive for high earners to stay in or move to the country with the progressive taxation. The clearest theoretical distillation of this argument is provided by \citet{kaplow2006undesirability}: 
under some technical assumptions, any redistribution achieved via a tax reform making commodity taxation progressive can be achieved more efficiently by instead equalizing tax rates across all goods and directly making the income taxation more progressive. 

The Kaplowian argument stands on firm grounds when discussing unilateral national tax policy in a given country. Importantly, the Kaplowian conclusion doesn't require work disincentive effects to be the primary force limiting the optimal taxation of income. In fact, there is a more straightforward argument in the case where international mobility of high earners is the primary force limiting the degree of progressivity that is optimal from national perspective with unilateral tax policy: progressive commodity taxation is similar to progressive income taxation in decreasing a country's attractiveness for high earners. So to maintain a given level of attractiveness, a country is best off taxing commodities at the same rate to leave consumption choices undistorted and to adjust the income tax to the highest level of progressivity compatible with maintaining the targeted level of attractiveness for the high earners.

However, when it comes to discussing optimal EU-wide taxes, these arguments have to be qualified. In fact, precisely because the progressivity of income taxation has been eroded due to tax competition for the internationally mobile high earners, particularly in the EU (\citet{kleven2011taxation}),
luxury good taxation can potentially play an important role in partially mitigating this lack of progressivity. It could be retorted that, again based on a Kaplowian perspective, there should instead be a progressive EU-wide income tax. However, agreeing on a common base for an income tax seems to be a daunting challenge. The complexity involved has been well recognized by the most prominent proposals for an EU wide additive tax on high incomes: \citet{hennette2019democratize}
proposes to create a new legislative body, parallel to the European Parliament, to deliberate on such complex fiscal questions. In comparison to this, agreeing on a set of goods to be defined as "luxury" for the purposes of an EU-wide additive tax for EU own resources seems relatively straightforward. 

Moreover, progressive commodity taxation has an added benefit compared to progressive income taxation: it also acts partly like a progressive tax on wealth, given that luxury spending is positively correlated with wealth and decreases the purchasing power of the wealth \citet{kaplow2008theory}.
Thus the lack of progressivity of wealth taxation arising from the international mobility of people with high wealth (\cite{agrawal2025wealth}) provides a further rationale for progressive commodity taxation. Importantly, this form of quasi-wealth redistribution does not affect incentives to save or invest, in contrast to an actual tax on wealth. As a result, many of the concerns that have led to opposition to wealth taxation (see e.g. the recent discussion in France) do not apply to luxury taxes. In addition, luxury taxation does not reduce private philanthropy, in contrast to progressive taxes on income and wealth.

Determining the optimal taxation of luxury goods based on these progressitivy-grounded considerations is a challenging task beyond the scope of this report. Instead, we will derive normatively optimal tax rate under a very conservative approach that disregards the arguments grounded in progressivity just made. Our approach, explained in the next section, focuses entirely on a different rationale for luxury taxation, namely signaling and status-externality effects. This approach, as we will see, highlights also that even if the optimal progressivity of income and wealth taxation was achieved at the EU level, substantial EU-wide additive luxury taxes would remain efficiency-enhancing. However, before explaining the optimal luxury taxation given signaling and status externality effects, we shall briefly discuss how developments in AI could alter the role of luxury taxation.


\subsection{The optimal taxation of status goods}

A further rationale for taxing luxury goods arises due to signaling and status externality effects. In the case of some luxury goods, there is strong evidence that a part of the motivation for purchasing the goods is derived from the information that it reveals to others (e.g. about the consumer's wealth/income, about the person's commitment in case of a gift). This information revelation still happens when the tax on the luxury goods gets increased. In the extreme case of a "pure diamond good" (\citet{ng1987diamonds}) 
where the information revelation is the only motive, any increase in the tax is beneficial as it results functionally in the same outcome (information transmission) with less resources being spent on the good. In the more realistic case of an impure diamond good where the information transmission motive co-exists with the intrinsic enjoyment of the good, there exists a finite optimal tax rate.

A related alternative model posits that luxury goods generate status benefits (e.g. via signaling of wealth or income) to the consumer but that these status benefits are fixed-sum in the aggregate. Thus luxury consumption creates a classical externality that needs a Pigovian tax for correction. It turns out that for empirically plausible elasticity values, the optimal tax rate on luxury goods is similar in this status externality model as in the partial diamond good model. For reasons of tractability, we report the results for the status externality model are derived by \citet{Xie2026}. 

The critical parameter for the calculation of the optimal tax on luxury goods turns out to be the answer to the following question: Per dollar spent on a luxury good, what is the number $\phi$ of dollars in benefits that a person derives from the status benefits that the consumption confers to her (e.g. via signaling of wealth and income)? In general, it is difficult to estimate this parameter. However, \cite{bursztyn2018status} exploits a rare opportunity to do that: in a randomized experiment, consumers were offered two types of functionally equivalent platinum credit cards, one type where this is visible and one type where it is not.  The experiment implies $\phi=1/3$.

From \citet{Xie2026}, we obtain the following simple formula for the optimal tax $t_{luxury}$ on luxury as a function of the tax $t_{other goods}$ prevailing on other goods, the prevailing markups and the status parameter $\phi$ which give how many dollars a person derives as status benefits per dollar spent on a luxury good: 

\[    1+t_{luxury}=(1+t_{other goods})\frac{1}{1-\phi}\frac{1+markup_{other goods}}{1+markup_{luxury}}\]
We obtain the result  $t_{luxury}=0.42$ for the following  estimates from \citet{Xie2026}:
\[
t_{other goods}=0.18 ,\phi=1/3
, markup_{other goods}=0.12,markup_{luxury}=0.4\]

Under this calibration, we thus obtain that luxury goods should be taxed at 42\%. This calculation has not taken into account the other above-mentioned rationales (beyond status effects) for taxing luxury goods. Therefore, it should be considered a conservative estimate for the optimal tax on luxury goods. If we were to account for the fact that the progressivity of wealth and income taxes has been eroded due to tax competition and that luxury good consumption is correlated with high income and wealth then we would arrive at potentially much higher estimates for the optimal tax on luxury goods. % AF we need to make some hypothesis here to account for it LS: accounting for this requires embedding commodity taxation in a model with tax competition for high earner/high wealth owners. \subsection{The self-interested case} % for the EU to tax luxury consumption at higher rates than other goods and services}

Our discussion so far has focused on the optimal taxation of luxury in a hypothetical globally coordinated policy setting. When informing EU-countries on what luxury taxation is in their collective interest, we have to take into account further effects, detailed below, arising 

The above-mentioned rationales for taxing luxury consumption at higher rates than other goods apply for each country individually. The population of each country benefits when status externalities (which plausibly arise primarily due to comparisons of people with a given country where they live) are taxed and thereby corrected.

However, there are three major additional effects that arise for any given country's calculation of how to set its luxury tax (on luxury purchases on its own territory) to maximize its own welfare:

1) The profits arising due to the mark-ups on the luxury goods and services partly accrue to people in other countries.

2) A part of the luxury consumption is done by visitors from other countries and they thus bear part of the tax burden.

3) Increases in the luxury tax causes people (domestic consumers and potential visitors) to go to another country for their luxury purchases.

The effects 1) and 2) create national incentives to set the luxury tax high. If effect 3), which pushes instead towards low tax rates was sufficiently weak then we would get the theoretical prediction that national governments have an overall incentive to set their luxury taxes above the globally optimal level. However,\citet{Xie2026} provides an empirically calibrated model (for portable personal luxury goods) that implies that 3) is actually stronger than 1) and 2) combined.  Thus we obtain the conclusion that tax competition distorts national luxury taxes downwards. This provides a strong rationale for a luxury tax at the EU level in order to correct for the forces of tax competition. Specifically, the modeling from \citet{Xie2026} yields that the marginal cost of raising a EUR via an EU-wide luxury tax, starting from the situation where the VAT refund has been abolished, is 0.88 EUR\footnote{It must be emphasized that there is large uncertainty associated with this estimate. We have not found any other estimate in the literature. The 0.88 estimate based on the model  \citet{Xie2026} is for a specific category of luxury (namely luxury leather goods) as a proof-of concept. The calibration can be straightforwardly extended to other categories with substantial additional data work.}. This estimate implies that it is 12 percent cheaper for the EU to raise funding via luxury taxation  than via GNI-based contributions. 

Importantly, this estimate that a marginal euro of revenue raised via an EU-wide luxury tax only costs the EU countries 0.88 euros already account for some of the main effects that are commonly (and justifiably) adduced as arguments against such taxes in Europe. Firstly, the underlying modeling captures the effects on the EU's luxury industry. The model captures this via a markup that accrues to the luxury producers that is higher than in other sectors due to the strong brand-based differentiation characteristic of the industry. 
Secondly, the model takes into account cross-border shopping diversion to countries such as the UK, Switzerland and the Gulf countries and the fact that such diversion reduces the EU countries' revenue from the VAT collected on the non-refundable consumption by the luxury shoppers.

\section{Effects on global welfare }

Some luxury goods such as jewelry have large negative externalities due to the role that precious stones play in financing conflicts and in undermining democratic accountability, as the large literature on the "resource curse" has documented. This adds a further reason for why the optimal tax rates on the relevant luxury goods can be expected to be larger than the one calculated (based on signaling/ status exernalities) above.

Moreover, luxury taxes in the EU could benefit other countries whose luxury tax revenue gets eroded via smuggling by travelers buying luxury goods in the EU: Currently, non-EU residents can get a full refund for their purchases of luxury goods in the EU when they return to their country of residence. This leads to high margins for people who smuggle luxury goods back to e.g. China to resell it there. Given that it is costly to extensively audit travelers, there are strong incentives for people to not declare their purchases to the customs, thereby evading the high taxes, particularly in China. This has led the Chinese government to reduce its luxury good tax rates \footnote{Like most of the countries outside of the EU, China has luxury taxes. Since 1994, China has levied a \emph{consumption tax} (CT) on 15 categories of
goods---including tobacco, alcohol, cosmetics, jewelery, luxury watches, yachts, and passenger vehicles---collected primarily at the production or import stage rather than at retail \citep{PwCChina2025,ChinaBriefingCT2024}.
For passenger cars, the CT is graduated by engine displacement, ranging from 1\%
for engines below 1.0\,L to 40\% for engines above 4.0\,L.
In addition, since December~2016, a 10\% surcharge applies to ``super-luxury''
vehicles above a price threshold, originally set at RMB\,1.3~million
(VAT-excluded) .
} in an attempt to reduce the incentives for smuggling. 

This suggests that by abolishing the VAT refunds for departing non-residents, the EU could enable other countries like China to tax luxury goods at the rate that they deem appropriate, thereby benefiting them. To see why, note that potential smugglers weigh the benefits of smuggling, i.e. the margin that they earn by smuggling and reselling in China, against the associated cost, i.e. the stress of doing something illegal and the (small) chance of being caught in audits. The removal of the VAT refunds in the EU would reduce the margin that smugglers earn, thus reducing the number of people who opt for smuggling. Moreover, China could increase its luxury good tax by the amount of the current EU VAT refund without increasing the net incentives (and thus the plausible net amount) of smuggling from the EU. 

It follows that removing EU VAT refunds would benefit other countries by allowing them to set their luxury good tax rates with less of the distortion created by the smuggling problem. Moreover, it would also directly increase economic welfare by reducing the extent of smuggling, thereby allowing the human labor currently expended on this illegal activity to be redirected to productive uses.




\section{Revenue estimates}

\subsection{Aggregate revenue estimate}

Our proposed excise involves category-specific price thresholds, with a uniform ad valorem rate being applied to the value exceeding the corresponding threshold. Defining these threshold for each category is beyond the scope of this report. Instead, we provide a rough estimate for the revenue potential of luxury taxes assuming, hypothetically that the thresholds are set at 0 but that the product categories are defined in a way corresponding to luxury product status in our data sources. Assuming that the threshold gets in practice set at a relatively low level, the results presented here should give a good approximation.

For each category, let us assume that pre-tax prices are unaffected by the luxury taxes. \footnote{This happens in monopolistic competition models, used e.g. by \citet{Xie2026} for the analysis of luxury good taxation.} Consider the case of a luxury good (e.g. luxury cars) that is bought only by residents. Let us assume that the demand has constant price elasticity denoted by $\epsilon$.  Denoting the pre-tax price of the good by $p$, the country $i$'s own ad valorem \footnote{This includes both VAT and excises if they exist.} tax on luxury goods by $t_{i}$ and the additive EU-wide VAT by $t_{EU}$. Then we can write the quantitiy consumed in terms of the status quo value, denoted $Q_i^0$:

\[
Q_i=Q_i^0 (\frac{1+t_i+t_{EU}}{1+t_i})^{\epsilon}
\]
Let $T_{i,EU}$ denote the revenue from the EU-wide tax collected by country $i$. We get:

\[
T_{i,EU}=t_{EU} p Q_i=t_{EU} p Q_i^0 (\frac{1+t_i+t_{EU}}{1+t_i})^{\epsilon}
\]
Now denoting by $R_i^0$ the status quo spending on the luxury good category by country $i$, we get $R_i^0=p Q_i^0 (1+t_i)$. Given our assumption that both the pre-tax price $p$ and the country's own tax rate are unaffected by the EU-wide additive ad valorem tax, we can thus write:

\[
T_{i,EU}=t_{EU} \frac{R_i^0}{1+t_i} (\frac{1+t_i+t_{EU}}{1+t_i})^{\epsilon}
\]
Rearranging gives:

\[
T_{i,EU}=\frac{t_{EU}}{1+t_i+t_{EU}} R_i^0 (\frac{1+t_i+t_{EU}}{1+t_i})^{1+\epsilon}

\paragraph{Calibrating the price elasticity of demand for luxury goods}

For portable personal luxury goods, arguably the most directly relevant tax-base elasticity comes from the UK Office for
Budget Responsibility's review of the abolition of the VAT Retail Export Scheme
\href{https://obr.uk/box/vat-retail-export-scheme-review-of-the-2020-policy-costing/}{(OBR,
2024)}. The OBR's original 2020 costing assumed an elasticity of $-1.9$, itself
a 50\% scale-up of a UK tourism elasticity of $-1.28$ intended to capture the
greater price-sensitivity of tax-free luxury shoppers; on review the OBR judged
this too high and revised its central estimate to approximately $-1.2$. It is
important to read this figure for what it measures: a \emph{tax-base} elasticity
that bundles the pure consumption response together with the diversion of
tourist purchases to competing destinations---above all to Paris and Milan,
which retained tax-free shopping when the UK withdrew it. The estimate is
therefore dominated by a \emph{destination-substitution} margin that is specific
to a unilateral national measure.

For an EU-wide luxury surcharge the relevant elasticity is likely to be
substantially smaller in absolute value. The $-1.2$ estimate is large precisely
because a non-EU visitor deciding where to buy a handbag could avoid the UK
price increase by purchasing in another European capital at no cost to their
itinerary; the tax fell on one destination while its closest substitutes
remained untaxed. A harmonised surcharge applied simultaneously across all 27
member states closes exactly this margin: intra-EU relocation of purchases no
longer avoids the tax, and only the two weaker channels remain---genuine
reductions in consumption, and diversion to destinations outside the EU
(principally the UK, Switzerland, and the Gulf and Asian luxury hubs). Since a
large share of the leakage embodied in the OBR figure is intra-European, the
own-price elasticity of the EU-wide tax base should be expected to lie well
below $1.2$ in magnitude, closer to the underlying consumption elasticity of
personal luxury goods, with only a modest additional wedge for extra-EU
diversion.

We can also get some theoretical prior on $\epsilon$: we know for "pure diamond goods" (\citet{ng1987diamonds}), i.e. goods where the only motivation is signaling via the spending, that $\epsilon=-1$. For the realistic case where consumers are also motivated in part by the intrinsic enjoyment of the good, the elasticity can be below or above $-1$. The econometric estimates find demand to be inelastic, i.e. less elastic than $1$ in absolute value:

Let us consider the case where $\epsilon=-1$ for each of the luxury categories. This assumption is conservative as far as the revenue potential is concerned: The evidence provided above suggests that $\epsilon >-1$ , which would imply more tax revenue from the EU-wide additive tax. Thus let us focus on the case $\epsilon=-1$. We get from the formula derived above the tax revenue from the EU-wide tax as a function of the status quo consumer spending $R_i^0$, the status quo national tax rate $t_i$  and the EU-wide tax rate $t_{EU}$, :
\[
T_{i,EU}= R_i^0 \frac{t_{EU}}{1+t_i+t_{EU}}
\]
We see that the amount of tax revenue that the EU-wide tax rate generates increases in the tax rate $t_{EU}$ and approaches $\$ 322billion$ as the tax rate goes to infinity. 

Let us now compute the tax revenue for the case where all EU country have a tax rate on luxury goods equal to the average statutory rate, i.e. $22\%$ (https://taxfoundation.org/data/all/eu/value-added-tax-vat-rates-europe/):

\[
\sum_{i\in {EU}} T_{i,EU}= \sum_{i\in {EU}} R_i^0 \frac{t_{EU}}{1+0.22+t_{EU}}= \$ 322 billion \frac{t_{EU}}{1.22+t_{EU}}
\]
Consider the case where we set $t_{EU}=0.2$ so that the overall ad valorem tax rate is equal to 42\%, the (conservatively estimated) optimal rate according to \citet{Xie2026}. Then we get for the aggregate revenue collected via the EU-wide luxury tax:
\[
\sum_{i\in {EU}} T_{i,EU}= \$ 322billion \frac{0.2}{1.42}=\$ 45 billion
\]

\paragraph{Further revenue potential}
The revenue estimate of $\$45billion$ that we just provided is conservative in at least two ways: Firstly, the (surprisingly weak) empirical evidence surveyed above is consistent with the demand actually being substantially less elastic than our assumed number of $\epsilon=-1$ . This would imply that the maximal theoretically achievable \textit{revenue potential} is larger than the $\$322$ 
 that we have for $\epsilon =-1$. 

Of course, the socially optimal tax on luxury goods is likely much below the rate that maximizes tax revenue. At some point, excessively high luxury tax rates could reduce work incentives for those of the high-earners that are motivated by the intrinsic enjoyment that they derive from consuming luxury goods. However, given that the tax progressivity in the EU has been pushed down by tax competition for people with high income and wealth, the optimal tax on luxury goods is likely substantially above the $42\%$ that is justified by status externalities alone. In fact, in the theoretical scenario where the price elasticity of overall luxury demand is close to 0, the luxury tax can make up for most of the lack of progressivity of the income and wealth taxation in the EU.

\subsection{Revenue collected by Member State } \label{tax revenue estimates by eu member states}

As motivated above, this report proposes the use of a uniform EU-wide tax rate across all luxury goods to fund the EU budget as an own resource. One important design choice implicit in this proposal is to bundle all the luxury tax bases together, rather than putting forward luxury taxes on specific categories as separate proposals. The following estimates provide one of the motivations for such a broadly bundled approach by showing that the tax revenue collected and thus contributed to the EU budget is not that far away to being proportional to GDP.

This purely descriptive finding suggests that the proposal has some promise for garnering unanimity if countries are well-informed about their self-interest, under two reasonable assumptions. Firstly, let us assume that the benefits from the EU spending is roughly proportional to GDP. This is plausible for international public goods like pandemic prevention and preparedness that the EU contributes to. Secondly, let us assume that the tax burden is roughly proportional to the tax revenue collected. This latter assumption is not necessarily accurate as there are other components in the overall tax burden, most notably profits and international shifting of consumers. However, the proportionality assumption likely provides a first decent approximation for the distribution of tax burdens across countries.

Under these two assumptions, it follows that at a given overall benefit cost ratio of the proposed reform (additive EU-wide luxury tax to fund EU budget) is more likely to garner unanimity if the ratios of tax revenue to GDP are less dispersed across countries. To see why, note that the impact of additional own resources is to either expand EU spending or to replace the residual contributions that are proportional GNI which itself is close to GDP. If all countries had the same ratio of tax revenue collected to GDP then rational voting behaviour would yield unanimity as long as the aggregate benefit cost ratio exceeds 1. This is highly plausible, as we will argue below.

\subsection{Should countries with high relative tax burden be compensated?}

A natural approach for compensating countries with high relative tax burden could be to reduce their required GNI-based contributions. If countries believe that EU own resources substitute 1 for 1 for GNI-based contributions then this  could be seen as favorable for facilitating unanimity. However, there are substantial downsides associated with such an approach arising from the fact that it would aggravate countries' tax competition for people with high wealth and income. To see why, consider the extreme case where the GNI-based contribution is fully adjusted so that each country ends up making a net contribution to the EU budget corresponding to its GNI share. In this case, a country de facto gets to fully keep every euro of additional luxury tax revenue, given that it gets compensated one for one for exceeding the tax revenue corresponding to its GNI share. The country would therefore have an incentive to lower its taxes on high income and high wealth due to the increased gain from attracting such people, given that their disproportionate spending on luxury goods.

The detrimental effect of compensating countries for high luxury tax contributions to the EU with reduced GNI-based contributions that we just highlighted scales with the size of the corresponding country's luxury tax base. A pragmatic approach is therefore to resort to this form of compensation for all countries who are sufficiently small in this sense and who are sufficiently disproportionately burdened by the luxury tax. Such an approach can increase the chances of reaching unanimity without compromising too much on the welfare gains that the reform can achieve.

\section{The case for using EU-wide luxury taxes as EU own resources}

Tax competition between EU member states undermines the unilateral incentives for EU countries to introduce and raise luxury taxes. This international spillovers suggest that there might be aggregate gains for EU member states to jointly increase luxury good taxes even if it is not in the individual interest of any country to do this individually. This motivates the proposal to introducing a new EU-wide ad valorem luxury excise. Empirically calibrated modeling (\citet{Xie2026}) suggests that there are already some countries who would benefit from unilaterally introducing luxury taxes. However, the collective economic gains from luxury taxes are much larger.

The introduction of such a new additive EU-wide luxury VAT\footnote{similarly for other taxes such as excises. Given the above-mentioned administrative cost advantages of VAT, we focus on the VAT implementation.} requires unanimity between member states for EU-wide adoption.\footnote{Enhanced cooperation could also be explored. However, luxury taxation becomes more costly if only a subset of EU countries participates, given the induced displacement of the luxury consumption to EU countries that do not participate.}  
For this it is, other things equal, favorable if the revenue raised creates large collective benefits. Thus it could be the case that using the tax revenue for the EU budget as an own resource, as we propose, could actually make it more likely that all member states vote for it.

\subsection{Bundling to facilitate unanimity} 

The proposed European Luxury Surcharge as an EU own resource purports to simultaneously address two current inefficiencies: 1) suboptimal luxury good tax rates driven by tax competition and 2) suboptimal funding for the EU (in particular EU-wide international public goods). 

Alternatively, one could have two separate EU-wide policy reforms, each addressing one of these problems: Firstly, one could have a scheme where an EU-wide luxury tax gets introduced and each member state gets to keep its revenue. Secondly, one could have a scheme where each member state has to contribute additional funding for the EU, e.g. in proportion to its GNI as in the traditional EU funding schem . One could call this alternative the "separated approach", with a separate vote for each of them at the EU level (each requiring unanimity).

This separation approach has been the dominant approach, both globally and at the EU level. For example, the Global Minimum Tax on corporate income does not impose any earmarking conditions. Instead, it focuses on addressing a race-to-the bottom in corporate income tax rates and, relatedly, the inefficiency of transfer-pricing based tax avoidance. Funding of global funds for global publics goods and redistribution is generated separately from any tax coordination. It is based on voluntary contributions and, in the case of the UN institutions, partly on assessed contributions calculated in proportion to GDP that countries have to pay to keep their voting rights at the UN general assembly.

Similarly, at the EU level, the separation approach has been the dominant one. Most existing mechanisms for tax coordination impose rules on member states without any earmarking obligations. For example, there are restrictions on the set of VAT rates that member states are allowed to impose. Mostly, tax revenues are retained by member states. Small exceptions include the EU-wide plastic own resource that both addresses an international externality and contributes to the EU budget. However, most of the funding for the EU budget comes from GNI-based contributions and tax-based contributions (e.g. the required 0.3\% of VAT tax base) do not (aim to) address tax competition or other under-taxation issues.

In the case of the EU, the separation approach might be motivated as follows. If the international public goods that the EU budget in part provides generate benefits that are roughly proportional to countries' GNI then funding them via GNI-based contributions (or VAT base contributions that themselves are roughly proportional to GNI) ensures that all countries are better off as a result, as long as the international public goods yield more than 1 EUR in aggregate benefits per 1 EUR spent on them, a condition synonymous with under-provision, which clearly is the case we are in.

However, such exact proportionality of the GNIs and the benefits that countries derive from additional spending by the EU is unlikely to hold in practice. This could explain the difficulties of getting agreement on expanding GNI-based contributions. Similarly, there might be some countries that will not gain from EU-wide luxury taxes if the revenue is retained nationally. However, if the opposing countries differ between the two components (increasing EU spending and increasing EU-wide luxury taxation), then the bundle might still garner unanimity.

\subsection{Bunddling to avoiding aggravating other tax competition}
A further argument in favor of the bundled approach exemplified by EU own resource design is that it can alleviate tax competition on other (potentially hard to contract on) tax instruments, in contrast to the separated approach. Specifically, consider the scenario where (under a separated approach) a minimum tax on luxury goods has been adopted at the EU level, where members states are obliged to impose it but can fully retain the tax revenue from it. Such a scheme would increase the value for each government of attracting top earners that buy luxury goods as fiscal residents, given that now the government will collect the luxury tax on this. As a result, the tax competition forces that depress top income tax rates in the EU (\citet{munoz2021european}, \citet{kleven2011taxation}) would get intensified.

This is in stark contrast to the case of the EU own resource. In that case, the new luxury tax revenue would not generate a strong additional incentive for EU countries to attract high earners. To see why, note firstly that each government in the EU will value a euro collected in revenue from the common luxury tax at typically much less than 1 euro, given that it cannot retain it and given that only a small part of the international public good benefits generated by the EU spending accrue to any given country. Moreover, if high earners move to other EU countries, they will also generate this luxury tax revenue for the EU budget, further reducing a given government concern for attracting high earners from other EU countries. As a result, the EU own resource design would not substantially aggravate top income tax competition within the EU, in contrast to the separated approach. % AF: Yes but the first order effects is foregone revenue for domestic purposes.
